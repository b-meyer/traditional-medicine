# Unani Medicine (Unani Tibb / Greco-Arabic Medicine): An In-Depth, Tertiary-Source-Based Report

## TL;DR
- Unani Tibb is a humoral, temperament-based medical system that began as Greek (Hippocratic-Galenic) medicine, was systematized and expanded by physicians of the medieval Islamic world (al-Razi, al-Majusi, Ibn Sina, Ibn al-Nafis, al-Zahrawi), and today survives chiefly in South Asia—formally institutionalized in India under the Ministry of AYUSH and recognized by the WHO, which issued benchmark documents for its training and practice in 2022.
- Its theoretical core—four humours (akhlat), temperament (mizaj), the seven "naturals" (umoor-e-tabiya), the healing power of nature (tabiyat), and the six essential lifestyle factors (asbab-e-sitta zaruriya)—is historically identical to the Galenic framework that Western scientific medicine abandoned in the 17th–19th centuries after Vesalius, Harvey, and germ theory; the humoral model has no standing in modern biomedical physiology.
- The best available tertiary/review evidence shows Unani's lifestyle and regimenal practices (diet, exercise, cupping) overlap with mainstream preventive medicine, but clinical-trial evidence for specific Unani drug therapies is weak (small, poorly controlled trials), and there are documented safety risks from heavy-metal-containing mineral formulations (kushta) and contamination; claims of efficacy should be treated cautiously.

## Key Findings

**1. Origins and lineage are well documented and non-controversial.** "Unani" derives from the Greek "Ionia"; the system's foundation is the humoral doctrine of Hippocrates (c. 460–370 BCE) and its systematic elaboration by Galen (129–c. 216 CE). Per *Encyclopædia Britannica* (a tertiary encyclopedia), "The origins of Unani medicine are found in the doctrines of the ancient Greek physicians Hippocrates and Galen," later "developed and refined through systematic experiment by the Arabs, most prominently by … Avicenna."

**2. The medieval Islamic contribution was transmission plus genuine advance.** Through the Greco-Arabic translation movement in Abbasid Baghdad (8th–10th c.), Greek medicine was rendered into Arabic—most importantly by Hunayn ibn Ishaq (d. 873) and his school—then extended by original clinicians: al-Razi (Rhazes) on smallpox/measles, al-Majusi (Haly Abbas) with the *Kamil al-Sina'a*, Ibn Sina (Avicenna) with the *Canon of Medicine*, al-Zahrawi (Albucasis) in surgery, and Ibn al-Nafis, who described the pulmonary circulation c. 1242.

**3. The theoretical architecture is coherent and internally systematic.** Unani physiology rests on seven "naturals" (al-umoor al-tabiya): elements (arkan), temperaments (mizaj), humours (akhlat), organs (aza), vital spirits (arwah), faculties (quwa), and functions (afaal). Health is the balance (i'tidal) of these, especially a person's normal mizaj; disease is their derangement. Prevention is governed by the six essential factors (asbab-e-sitta zaruriya), and healing is attributed to the body's own tabiyat (vis medicatrix naturae).

**4. Diagnosis is clinical and observational**, centered on pulse (nabz), and inspection of urine (baul) and stool (baraz), supplemented by history and physical examination guided by the ten categories (ajnas-e-ashara).

**5. Four treatment modalities**: regimenal therapy (ilaj bil tadbeer—cupping/hijama, venesection/fasd, leeching, massage, bath, exercise, etc.), dietotherapy (ilaj bil ghiza), pharmacotherapy (ilaj bil dawa—single and compound drugs graded by temperament and four degrees of potency), and surgery (ilaj bil yad), historically a last resort.

**6. Current status**: India is the institutional heartland (Ministry of AYUSH; regulation via the National Commission for Indian System of Medicine, formerly the Central Council of Indian Medicine; research via CCRUM). Pakistan, Iran (as Traditional Persian Medicine), Bangladesh and others also sustain it. The WHO recognized Unani in 1976 and published benchmark documents for training and practice in 2022.

**7. Scientific evaluation is mixed-to-skeptical**: the humoral framework is scientifically obsolete; clinical evidence for Unani interventions is generally low-quality; and heavy metals in some formulations pose real toxicity risks.

## Details

### 1. History and Origins

**Greek foundations (tertiary/encyclopedic).** The *Encyclopædia Britannica* entry on Unani medicine states the system's origins lie in Hippocrates and Galen and that health is maintained by the balance of seven physiological principles (al-umoor al-tabiyah) and four humours (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile). Galen (129–c. 216 CE) supplied the systematic humoral-temperamental synthesis, extensive pharmacology, and the anatomical framework that dominated for over a millennium. The word "Unani" itself traces to the Greek place-name Ionia.

**The Greco-Arabic translation movement (scholarly monograph + encyclopedia).** According to Dimitri Gutas's *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture: The Graeco-Arabic Translation Movement in Baghdad and Early ʿAbbāsid Society* (Routledge, 1998)—the standard academic authority—the systematic translation of Greek medical and scientific works into Arabic occurred in Baghdad in the 2nd–4th/8th–10th centuries under Abbasid patronage. The central figure was Hunayn ibn Ishaq (Johannitius, d. 873), a Nestorian Christian scholar whose translations of Galen and Hippocrates (as well as Plato and Aristotle) made Greek medicine available in Arabic; the standard reference biography is Gotthard Strohmaier's entry "Ḥunayn b. Isḥāḳ" in the *Encyclopaedia of Islam*, 2nd edition (Brill). **Important scholarly caveat:** specialist scholarship (Gutas; Gutas & van Bladel, "Bayt al-ḥikma," *Encyclopaedia of Islam THREE*, 2009) cautions against the popular romanticized image of the "House of Wisdom" (Bayt al-Hikma) as a grand translation academy; the historical record supports a more modest palace library/archive, and the translation movement was broader than and largely independent of that single institution.

**Key figures (peer-reviewed reviews + reference works):**
- **Abu Bakr al-Razi / Rhazes (865–925)**: Persian polymath, called "the Arab Galen." His *Kitab al-Jadari wa'l-Hasba* (Treatise on Smallpox and Measles) is regarded as the first clinical differentiation of the two diseases; his encyclopedic *al-Hawi* (Latin *Liber Continens*) summarized the medical knowledge of his time. He was willing to criticize Galen. (*Encyclopedia.com*'s "The Medical Influence of Rhazes" is a tertiary reference entry; peer-reviewed narrative reviews corroborate his clinical priority.)
- **Ali ibn al-Abbas al-Majusi / Haly Abbas (d. c. 994)**: Per Lutz Richter-Bernburg's entry "ʿAlī b. ʿAbbās Majūsī" in the *Encyclopaedia Iranica* (1985)—a peer-reviewed scholarly encyclopedia—he was "probably the most important medical writer between Rāzī and Ebn Sīnā."[S1] His encyclopedia *Kitab Kamil al-Sina'a al-Tibbiyya* (also *al-Kitab al-Maliki*; Latin *Liber Regius / Regalis Dispositio*), dedicated to the Buyid ruler ʿAḍud al-Dawla, was "soon superseded by Ebn Sīnā's *Qānūn*" but remained esteemed for its practical portions; it entered Europe via Constantine the African's *Liber Pantegni* and Stephen of Antioch's 1127 translation.
- **Ibn Sina / Avicenna (980–1037)**: His *al-Qanun fi al-Tibb* (*Canon of Medicine*, completed 1025) is a five-book encyclopedic synthesis of Greek, Roman, Persian and Indian medicine that became the standard medical textbook in both the Islamic world and European universities into the early modern period (used in Italy and elsewhere into the 17th century).
- **Ibn al-Nafis (1213–1288)**: Per John B. West's peer-reviewed review "Ibn al-Nafis, the pulmonary circulation, and the Islamic Golden Age" (*Journal of Applied Physiology*, 2008), in his *Sharh Tashrih al-Qanun* (Commentary on Anatomy in Avicenna's Canon), written ~1242, Ibn al-Nafis stated categorically that the interventricular septum is not porous and that blood must pass from the right ventricle to the left via the lungs—refuting Galen.[S2] The standard reference-work biography is Albert Z. Iskandar's "Ibn al-Nafīs" in the *Dictionary of Scientific Biography*. **Caveat:** his work was rediscovered only in 1924[S3] (Meyerhof, *Isis*, 1935), so it had negligible influence on European discoverers (Servetus, Colombo, Harvey).
- **Al-Zahrawi / Albucasis (936–1013)**: Andalusian surgeon; his 30-volume *Kitab al-Tasrif* included a surgical volume with ~200 illustrated instruments, and its Latin translation was a standard European surgical text for ~500 years. He is widely called the "father of surgery."

**Spread to South Asia and the Mughal era (scholarly + tertiary).** Unani arrived in India with Muslim rule roughly a millennium ago and flowered under the Mughals; per a peer-reviewed *Modern Asian Studies* (Cambridge) article, it "arrived in India with the Muslims and evolved with the expansion of the Mughal Empire." Emperors including Akbar, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb patronized hakims, hospitals (dar-ul-shifa), and translation of medical texts. Indian hakims added local drugs, producing a distinctively Indian Unani.

**Colonial decline and revival.** With the decline of the Mughal Empire and the rise of colonial biomedicine, Unani lost state patronage. Its early-20th-century revival is associated with **Hakim Ajmal Khan (1868–1927)**, of the Sharif Manzil (Ballimaran, Delhi) lineage, who founded the Ayurvedic and Unani Tibbia College in Delhi, promoted research, and (per Britannica) advocated scientific investigation of Unani natural products. His collaborator, phytochemist Salimuzzaman Siddiqui, isolated reserpine and other alkaloids from *Rauwolfia serpentina* in the 1930s, naming ajmaline/ajmalicine in his honor. The **Hamdard** enterprise, founded 1906 in Delhi by Hakim Hafiz Abdul Majeed, and later Hamdard (Pakistan) under Hakim Mohammed Said, became a major producer of Unani products.

### 2. Theoretical Foundations

**The seven naturals (umoor-e-tabiya).** Unani conceives human physiology through seven "natural" components: (1) **arkan** (four elements—earth, water, air, fire); (2) **mizaj** (temperament); (3) **akhlat** (four humours); (4) **aza** (organs); (5) **arwah** (vital spirits/pneuma); (6) **quwa** (faculties/powers); and (7) **afaal** (functions). Per Britannica and multiple scoping reviews, health is maintained by their balance.

**Humours (akhlat).** The four humours—dam (blood), balgham (phlegm), safra (yellow bile), sauda (black bile)—are produced in digestion and possess paired qualities (hot/cold, wet/dry). A person's balance of humours determines constitution and, when deranged, disease.

**Temperament (mizaj).** Reviews (e.g., a scoping review in the *Journal of Innovations in Applied Pharmaceutical Science*) describe mizaj as second in importance only to arkan; it classifies individuals, foods, and drugs into qualitative types (hot-dry/choleric, hot-wet/sanguine, cold-wet/phlegmatic, cold-dry/melancholic). Temperament is assessed through ten parameters (ajnas-e-ashara), including touch (malmas), flesh/fat, hair, complexion, physique, sleep/wakefulness, functions, wastes, and psychic reactions.

**Tabiyat (medicatrix naturae).** The body's own healing power (tabiyat) is considered the supreme agent maintaining physiological function; the Hippocratic dictum "the physician treats, but nature (tabiyat) heals" is central. The physician's role is to assist tabiyat.

**Six essential factors (asbab-e-sitta zaruriya).** Health preservation depends on managing six unavoidable factors: hawa (air/environment), ma'kul wa mashrub (food and drink), harkat wa sukun-e-badani (bodily activity and rest), harkat wa sukun-e-nafsani (mental activity and rest), naum wa yaqza (sleep and wakefulness), and ihtibas wa istifragh (retention and evacuation). Reviews increasingly map these onto modern lifestyle/preventive medicine for non-communicable diseases.

### 3. Diagnosis

Diagnosis relies on observation and physical examination. The three signature tools are **nabz** (pulse), **baul** (urine inspection), and **baraz** (stool inspection), supplemented by history and examination.[S4] Classical texts (Ibn Sina, al-Razi, al-Tabari) described the pulse under multiple parameters (rate, rhythm, force, volume) and enumerated factors influencing it (age, sex, temperament, season, food, sleep, exercise, emotion, pregnancy). Uroscopy is prominent in Avicenna's Canon. These techniques are functionally analogous to (but far less precise than) modern vital-sign and urinalysis diagnostics.

### 4. Treatment Modalities

**Ilaj bil tadbeer (regimenal therapy).** Per a peer-reviewed review in the *Journal of Complementary and Integrative Medicine* (De Gruyter, PubMed), nearly 30 regimens are described in classical Unani literature, including fasd (venesection), hijama (cupping, wet and dry), ta'liq al-'alaq (leech therapy), ishal (purgation), qay' (emesis), idrar (diuresis), huqna (enema), ta'riq (diaphoresis), riyazat (exercise), dalk (massage), hammam (bath), natul (irrigation), inkibab (steam), and takmid (fomentation). The aim is to evacuate morbid humours and restore balance.

**Ilaj bil ghiza (dietotherapy).** Considered the simplest, most natural approach; diet is adjusted in quantity and quality according to temperament and illness (Britannica cites a low-roughage, nutrient-rich diet for fever).

**Ilaj bil dawa (pharmacotherapy).** Britannica notes the Unani pharmacopoeia has "more than 2,000 medicines" of herbal, mineral, and animal origin, used singly (mufradat) or compounded (murakkabat). Drugs are classified by temperament (mizaj-e-advia) and by four **degrees** of potency; per a *Journal of Ethnopharmacology* article on islah-e-advia, third- and fourth-degree drugs are potentially toxic and require purification/processing (tadbir) before use, and correctives (musleh) and substitutes (abdal) are used to manage toxicity.

**Ilaj bil yad (surgery).** Historically a last resort and generally outside the hakim's routine practice, though al-Zahrawi's tradition shows a rich medieval surgical heritage.

### 5. Current Status

**India (institutional heartland).** Unani is one of the systems under the Ministry of AYUSH (an independent ministry since November 9, 2014; formerly the Department of ISM&H established 1995). Education and practice were historically regulated by the Central Council of Indian Medicine (statutory body under the Indian Medicine Central Council Act, 1970), now replaced by[S5] the National Commission for Indian System of Medicine (NCISM Act, 2020). The undergraduate degree is the Kamil-e-Tib-o-Jarahat (BUMS); postgraduate degrees include Mahir-e-Tib and Mahir-e-Jarahat. Research is coordinated by the Central Council for Research in Unani Medicine (CCRUM). India had **47,683 registered Unani practitioners**, per Minister of State (Independent Charge) for AYUSH Shripad Yesso Naik's April 28, 2015 Rajya Sabha reply (reported by Business Standard/PTI): "there are 47,683 Unani, 8,173 Siddha and 1,764 Naturopathy practitioners registered in the country." It has **55 Unani colleges**, per then-Health Minister Dr Harsh Vardhan's Lok Sabha data (reported by Medical Dialogues): of 733 total AYUSH institutes, "there are 414 Ayurveda based, 248 Homeopathy, 55 Unani, 13 Siddha, and 3 Sowa Rigpa based colleges."

**Pakistan** sustains a large body of hakims practicing Tibb-e-Unani, with institutions such as Hamdard University; the WHO/EMRO Hamdard Foundation Pakistan document "Unani Medicine: From Wellness to Wellbeing in Pakistan" states: "There are ~60,000 registered Hakims in Pakistan holding either diploma in Tibb-e-Unani or a Bachelor degree in Eastern/Unani medicine."

**Iran (Traditional Persian Medicine).** Closely related; based on the same four-humour and mizaj framework transmitted via al-Razi and Ibn Sina. It has been revived as a specialized academic discipline in Iranian universities of medical sciences, though peer-reviewed accounts note implementation challenges (overwork, underfunding, integration difficulties).

**WHO recognition.** Britannica states Unani "gained recognition from the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1976."[S6] In 2022 the WHO published two normative "benchmark" documents—one for the *training* and one for the *practice* of Unani medicine—updating a 2010 edition, defining minimum criteria for WHO Member States and aligned with the WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy 2014–2023.

### 6. Scientific Evidence and Critical Evaluation

**The humoral framework is scientifically obsolete.** Multiple history-of-medicine sources (including the AMA *Journal of Ethics* and the Royal College of Physicians museum) document that the four-humour theory—the shared foundation of Unani, Galenic and Traditional Persian medicine—was progressively dismantled by Vesalius's anatomy (1543), Harvey's demonstration of blood circulation (1628), microscopy, and germ theory (Pasteur, Koch), and was largely abandoned as scientific medicine by the 19th century. Modern endocrinology and biochemistry replaced humoral explanations entirely. Thus mizaj/akhlat have no correspondence to validated physiology; attempts in the Unani literature to "map" humours onto hormones, cytokines, or autonomic tone are speculative analogies, not established science.

**Clinical-trial evidence is weak.** Systematic reviews of Unani interventions consistently report few, small, poorly controlled studies. For example, a systematic review of Unani treatment of vitiligo (baras) found that of 63 articles only 13 were clinical trials, most non-randomized/uncontrolled, and concluded "appropriately designed, high quality clinical trials were not carried out." A systematic review/evidence map of Unani medicine in post-COVID-19 recovery found[S7] only 10 studies met inclusion criteria, with small samples (n≈60–100) and high risk of bias. Reviews of hijama (cupping) report some short-term benefit (e.g., for nonspecific low back pain) but call for rigorous, larger, controlled trials. The overall pattern mirrors the broader traditional-medicine literature: promising but low-certainty evidence.

**Safety concerns are real and documented.** Britannica's own entry flags that Unani *kushta*—incinerated powders prepared from toxic metals such as mercury (seemab), arsenic (sam al-far), mercuric chloride (sangraf) and iron—"may be effective, but they have significant toxic side effects." For the closely allied Indian systems, the peer-reviewed literature is stark: Saper et al. (JAMA 2004) found heavy metals in a significant fraction of Ayurvedic herbal products, and Saper RB, Phillips RS, Sehgal A, et al. (JAMA 2008;300(8):915–923) reported that "One-fifth [20.7%] of both US-manufactured and Indian-manufactured Ayurvedic medicines purchased via the Internet contain detectable lead, mercury, or arsenic" (rasa shastra ~41% vs non-rasa shastra ~17%); that same study noted "since 1978 more than 80 cases of lead poisoning associated with Ayurvedic medicine use have been reported worldwide." Numerous case reports document severe lead poisoning (blood lead levels exceeding 100 μg/dL) from such products. Because Unani shares the practice of deliberately incorporating minerals/metals, the same risks apply. Contamination with heavy metals, aflatoxins, pesticides, and microbial load is a recognized quality-control problem in herbal medicine generally, and herb-drug interactions are an additional concern.

**Research-quality critique.** Much of the Unani research literature is published in low-visibility or non-indexed journals, is descriptive or conceptual rather than experimental, and often frames studies to "validate" classical claims rather than to test them rigorously. This introduces publication and confirmation bias. The credible core—demonstrable pharmacological activity of specific plant constituents (e.g., reserpine from *Rauwolfia*)—shows that ethnopharmacological leads can be scientifically fruitful when subjected to modern isolation, standardization and trials, which is precisely what most Unani formulations still lack.

### 7. Relationship to Other Systems

- **Traditional Persian Medicine (TPM/ITM)** is essentially the same humoral system, drawing on al-Razi and Ibn Sina; "Persian medicine (also known as Islamic medicine, Unani, and Iranian traditional medicine)" is described in the literature as a single temperament-based tradition, now revived academically in Iran.
- **Ayurveda** is an independent Indian system with a parallel but distinct humoral framework (tridosha: vata, pitta, kapha). In India the two coexisted, cross-fertilized (Unani hakims adopted Indian drugs; some scholars posited early Greek-Indian exchange), and are jointly regulated under AYUSH. Both share the practice of using mineral/metal preparations and therefore similar safety concerns.
- **Modern European medicine's humoral heritage**: Western scientific medicine descends from the very same Hippocratic-Galenic humoralism, transmitted back to Europe through Arabic texts (Avicenna's Canon, al-Majusi's Liber Regius, al-Zahrawi's surgery). Europe abandoned the humoral model during the Scientific Revolution; Unani preserved and continued it. Thus Unani and biomedicine are historical siblings that diverged around the 17th century.

## Recommendations

**For a general reader or patient:**
1. **Treat Unani as a lifestyle-and-preventive tradition with historical, not biomedical, validity.** Its dietary moderation, exercise, sleep hygiene, and stress management (asbab-e-sitta zaruriya) are broadly consistent with mainstream preventive medicine and are low-risk.
2. **Do not rely on Unani (or Ayurvedic) mineral/metal formulations (kushta/bhasma).** These carry documented heavy-metal poisoning risk. If you use any traditional product, buy only from regulated manufacturers, avoid internet-sourced products, and tell your physician—especially if pregnant, or if you develop unexplained anemia or abdominal pain (possible lead toxicity). **Threshold to seek testing:** any unexplained anemia with basophilic stippling, or GI/neurological symptoms while taking such products, warrants a blood lead level.
3. **For serious, acute, or infectious disease, use evidence-based biomedicine.** Reserve Unani interventions, if used at all, as an adjunct—not a replacement—and only after discussion with a qualified clinician.

**For researchers/policymakers:**
4. **Prioritize rigorous, registered RCTs and standardized, contaminant-screened formulations.** The credible path (as reserpine demonstrated) is isolating and testing specific constituents. Fund pharmacovigilance and mandatory heavy-metal/aflatoxin/pesticide screening (ICP-MS) with enforced pharmacopoeial limits.
5. **What would change the assessment:** consistent, well-powered, low-risk-of-bias RCTs (and Cochrane-grade systematic reviews) showing specific Unani interventions outperform placebo/standard care for defined conditions would upgrade specific therapies from "traditional/unproven" to "evidence-based." Absent that, treat efficacy claims as unproven.

## Caveats
- **Source-type note (per the user's requirement):** The strongest tertiary/peer-reviewed anchors used here are the *Encyclopædia Britannica* entry on Unani medicine (tertiary encyclopedia); the *Encyclopaedia Iranica* entry on al-Majusi and *Encyclopaedia of Islam* entries on Hunayn ibn Ishaq and Bayt al-Hikma (peer-reviewed scholarly encyclopedias); Gutas's *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture* (scholarly monograph); West (2008, *J. Appl. Physiol.*) and Meyerhof (1935, *Isis*) on Ibn al-Nafis (peer-reviewed history-of-medicine articles); Iskandar in the *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* (scholarly reference); and multiple peer-reviewed review/systematic-review articles on mizaj, regimenal therapy, drug degrees, and efficacy. Where claims rest on **non-tertiary or non-peer-reviewed sources**—Indian government/AYUSH and WHO documents (primary/institutional, not peer-reviewed), news media (parliamentary statistics), practitioner websites, and predatory/low-impact journals—this has been flagged in-text. Practice statistics in particular derive from government/press sources (2015 parliamentary answers) and vary by year.
- Historical "firsts" attributed to Islamic-era physicians (e.g., al-Zahrawi as "father of surgery," al-Razi's smallpox priority) are widely repeated but sometimes hagiographic; the substantive, well-attested claims (Ibn al-Nafis on pulmonary circulation; the Canon's centuries-long influence) rest on solid scholarship.
- This report describes what Unani claims and what the evidence shows; it is not medical advice. Regulatory status and statistics are current as best available and may have changed after the cited dates.
