# Unani Medicine (Unani Tibb / Greco-Arabic Medicine): A Critical Research Report

## TL;DR
- **Unani is a genuine Greco-Arabic humoral tradition with a continuous textual lineage from Hippocrates and Galen through Abbasid Baghdad to South Asia — but its *modern institutional form* (colleges, standardized pharmacopoeias, "scientized" curricula incorporating anatomy and biomedicine) is substantially a late-19th/20th-century reconstruction, a point on which most serious historians (Attewell, Alavi, Liebeskind, Speziale) agree.**
- **On its own humoral terms the system is internally coherent, but its core diagnostic act — assessing mizaj (temperament) — has only slight-to-moderate inter-rater reliability (weighted kappa/ICC roughly 0.4–0.64 in the best Iranian and Indian studies), and the highest-quality clinical evidence for its interventions is weak, small, and at high risk of bias.**
- **Unani is legally recognized and state-funded in India (Ministry of AYUSH, NCISM Act 2020, CCRUM) and Pakistan (UAH Act 1965), with lesser recognition in Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, South Africa (11th allied health profession, 2001) and a parallel "Persian medicine" revival in Iran; documented harms include over-limit heavy metals in kushta/surma preparations, undeclared steroids, and hijama infections.**

## Key Findings

1. **Origins and texts are well-attested; dating/authorship debates are localized, not existential.** The tradition descends from the Hippocratic corpus and Galen, transmitted via the 8th–10th-century Abbasid translation movement (Hunayn ibn Ishaq's Risala enumerates 129 Galenic titles, of which he personally translated about 100 into Syriac and/or Arabic), synthesized in al-Razi's al-Hawi, al-Majusi's Kamil al-sina'a, and above all Ibn Sina's al-Qanun fi al-Tibb (completed c. 1025). Specialists broadly agree on this genealogy. Genuine scholarly uncertainty concentrates on (a) manuscript transmission and the lack of a complete critical edition of the Qanun, (b) apocryphal/pseudonymous attributions, and (c) the degree of originality vs. compilation.

2. **The explanatory framework is a coherent qualitative physiology that deliberately resists biomedical reduction.** Health = equilibrium of the four akhlat (humours: dam/blood, balgham/phlegm, safra/yellow bile, sauda/black bile) expressed as an individual's mizaj (temperament) across hot/cold and wet/dry axes, governed by arkan (elements), quwa (faculties), arwah (pneuma/spirits), tabiat (the body's self-healing physis), and maintained by the asbab sitta daruriya (six essentials: air, food/drink, movement/rest, sleep/wakefulness, retention/evacuation, mental states). It maps loosely onto biomedicine in areas like regimen, diet, and some materia medica, but its central constructs (mizaj, tabiat, humours) have no biomedical equivalent and are explicitly qualitative/individual rather than quantitative/populational.

3. **Diagnosis rests on pulse (nabz), urine (baul), stool, and temperament assessment — and agreement between examiners is modest.** Reliability studies exist and are worth reporting: Iranian temperament-questionnaire validation found weighted kappa 0.40–0.82 for retained items; expert-vs-expert ICC of 0.62 (warm-cold) and 0.64 (wet-dry); and a Persian-medicine study found clinical-judgment-vs-questionnaire agreement of only kappa ~0.51 at best and as low as 0.08 for balanced dry/wet. This is "slight to moderate" agreement by standard kappa benchmarks.

4. **Colonialism nearly killed it; survival required "scientization."** The Bhore Committee (1946) dismissed indigenous systems as unscientific; the Chopra Committee (1948) recommended integration. Reformers led by Hakim Ajmal Khan (founder of the Ayurvedic and Unani Tibbia College, Delhi, inaugurated 1921) deliberately incorporated anatomy, surgery and Western concepts — against the "purist" Lucknow (Azizi) school. The Hamdard institutions (Abdul Majeed 1906; Hakim Abdul Hameed in India, Hakim Mohammed Said in Pakistan) industrialized and standardized Unani pharmacy.

5. **Legal standing is strongest in India and Pakistan.** India: NCISM Act 2020 (replacing the 1970 IMCC Act, effective 2021), Ministry of AYUSH, CCRUM, Drugs and Cosmetics Act Chapter IVA, Unani Pharmacopoeia of India. Pakistan: Unani, Ayurvedic and Homoeopathic Practitioners Act 1965 and the National Council for Tibb. South Africa recognizes Unani-Tibb under the Allied Health Professions Act (registered 2001/2007). Iran runs a state-backed "Persian medicine" PhD track (since 2007). WHO issued benchmarks for the training and practice of Unani medicine (2022).

6. **Clinical evidence is abundant in volume but weak in quality.** Most trials come from CCRUM, Indian Unani colleges, and Iranian universities; systematic reviews consistently find small samples, high/unclear risk of bias, single-arm or open-label designs, and publication bias. Cupping (hijama) has the largest evidence base among procedures, with some low-to-moderate-quality positive signals for pain.

7. **Documented harms are real, especially metallic preparations and hijama.** Kushta (calx) and surma preparations have shown over-limit lead; undeclared steroids have been found in Unani products; hijama has caused serious infections and at least one reported death.

8. **"Invented tradition" vs. continuity is the central historiographic debate.** Attewell, Alavi, Liebeskind, Hardiman, and Speziale document extensive 19th–20th-century reconstruction; defenders emphasize genuine textual continuity.

9. **Borrowing from neighbouring traditions is textually demonstrated, not merely convergent.** Indian drugs entered Arabic/Persian pharmacopoeias; Ayurvedic texts were translated into Persian (Speziale's work on the "Persianised" Ayurveda genre).

10. **Current fights: evidence standards, AYUSH "mixopathy," the Persian-medicine revival, COVID-19 claims, and nomenclature.**

## Details

### 1. Origin, foundational texts, and scholarly consensus on dating/authorship/transmission

Unani ("Ionian/Greek") medicine is the South and Central Asian branch of the Greco-Arabic humoral tradition. Its intellectual genealogy is one of the best-documented in the history of medicine and enjoys broad specialist consensus on its main outline: the Hippocratic corpus (5th–4th c. BCE) and the systematizing work of Galen (129–c.216 CE) form the theoretical base; this Greek material was translated into Syriac and Arabic during the Abbasid translation movement of the 8th–10th centuries, centered on Baghdad and drawing on the earlier Sassanid academy at Gondishapur/Jundishapur.

The translation movement is documented in unusual detail because of Hunayn ibn Ishaq (808–873), the Nestorian Christian physician-translator whose own Risala (edited by Bergsträsser 1925; re-edited and translated by John Lamoreaux, 2016, *Hunayn ibn Ishaq on His Galen Translations*) enumerates 129 Galenic titles, of which he personally translated about 100 into Syriac and/or Arabic (per the Encyclopaedia of Islam entry, using Bergsträsser's standard numbering). Scholars including Dimitri Gutas (who stressed the Abbasids' political-ideological motives) and A. I. Sabra (who called the process the "appropriation and naturalisation" of Greek science) have analyzed the movement's mechanics.

The synthesizing Arabic-era texts that became the Unani canon are: al-Razi's (Rhazes, 865–925) encyclopedic *al-Hawi* (Liber Continens); Ali ibn al-Abbas al-Majusi's (Haly Abbas, d. 994) *Kamil al-sina'a al-tibbiyya* (also *Kitab al-Maliki*); and, above all, Ibn Sina's (Avicenna, 980–1037) *al-Qanun fi al-Tibb* (The Canon of Medicine), begun c. 1012 and completed between 1020 and 1025 across Gurgan, Rayy and Hamadan. The Qanun's five books organized general principles, simple drugs, localized diseases, general diseases, and compound remedies, and dominated both Islamic and Latin-European medical education for centuries.

**Where specialists actually disagree** is narrower than the popular framing suggests. First, textual/manuscript transmission of the Qanun is genuinely unsettled: there is still no complete modern critical edition of the Arabic text, and scholars such as Jules Janssens have noted that even the 2018 critical edition of part of Book I leaves "the larger unresolved textual problem."[S1] The earliest extant manuscripts are fragmentary or late (e.g., an Aga Khan Museum copy of Book 5 dated 444 H/1052 CE; the US National Library of Medicine's MS A 53 is a complete but undated Timurid-era copy). Second, there is a robust scholarly literature on apocryphal and pseudonymous attributions — e.g., texts ascribed to Avicenna (the *Tuhfat al-ashiqin*) or to Ja'far al-Sadiq that are not genuinely theirs, as documented by Speziale and others. Third, historians of the Qanun (e.g., the Historia Medica project, Nahyan Fancy's study of Fakhr al-Din al-Razi) emphasize that the Canon's importance lies less in originality than in how "copyists, commentators, translators, teachers, printers, and readers repeatedly selected, criticised, and changed"[S1] it — with more than two dozen Arabic commentaries from the 12th–14th centuries alone.[S1] Transmission to Persia and to Mughal and Deccan India (and the emergence of the "Unani" label there) is discussed under Details §8–9.

### 2. The explanatory framework and its (non-)mapping onto biomedicine

Unani physiology is a qualitative, individualized system built on nested constructs:
- **Arkan** (four elements: fire, air, water, earth), whose qualities (hot/cold, wet/dry) underlie everything.
- **Akhlat** (four humours): dam (blood, hot-wet), balgham (phlegm, cold-wet), safra (yellow bile, hot-dry), sauda (black bile, cold-dry).
- **Mizaj** (temperament): the resultant balance of qualities unique to each person, organ, drug and food; health is the maintenance of one's proper mizaj.
- **Quwa** (faculties/powers: natural, vital, psychic), **arwah** (pneuma/vital spirits), and **tabiat** (the body's innate self-regulating/healing power, equivalent to physis).
- **Asbab sitta daruriya** (six essential/non-natural causes governing health): (1) air/environment, (2) food and drink, (3) movement and rest, (4) sleep and wakefulness, (5) retention and evacuation, and (6) psychic/emotional states.

Disease is a disturbance of mizaj (su' al-mizaj), either simple (qualitative) or material (involving a morbid humour). Therapy proceeds by contraries (a cold disease treated with hot-quality drugs/regimen) and by supporting tabiat, using a hierarchy of regimen (ilaj bil-tadbir), diet (ilaj bil-ghiza), pharmacotherapy (ilaj bil-dawa) and, last, surgery (ilaj bil-yad).

**Mapping onto biomedicine**: The framework maps partially and loosely. Regimen and dietetics overlap with modern lifestyle medicine; much of the materia medica is being screened pharmacologically; some disease categories have rough biomedical analogues (e.g., Waja' al-Mafasil ≈ rheumatoid/joint disease, as Unani researchers themselves note). But the core theoretical objects — mizaj, the four humours, tabiat, arwah — have **no biomedical referents** and are explicitly individual and qualitative. The system therefore "refuses" mapping precisely where it matters most: it classifies patients and drugs by temperament rather than by pathophysiology or molecular mechanism, which is why standardization for research (see §3, §6) is so difficult.

### 3. Diagnosis and inter-rater reliability (with kappa values)

A hakim diagnoses through history plus physical signs: **nabz** (pulse — rate, rhythm, volume, character), **baul** (urine — colour, turbidity, sediment, odour), **baraz** (stool), and a global **mizaj assessment** synthesizing physique, skin, hair, sleep, digestion, psychological disposition, and the quality of wastes.

The user specifically asked for inter-rater reliability / agreement studies. **These exist**, principally from the Iranian "Persian medicine" revival and from Indian/Bangladeshi Unani researchers building mizaj questionnaires, and they are the most quantitatively honest window into the system's reproducibility:

- **Mojahedi et al. (Iranian Red Crescent Medical Journal 2014;16(3):e15924, mizaj self-report scale validation):** "The weighted kappa coefficients of the 39 items were between 0.40 and 0.82... Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.71." Cut-off sensitivity/specificity were modest: warm group 65%/93%; cold 52%/97%; dry 53%/67%; wet 53%/76% — note the poor sensitivity for the dry/wet axis.
- **Expert-vs-expert agreement (three Persian-medicine specialists, 150 volunteers):** Intraclass Correlation Coefficient **0.62 (CI 0.53–0.73) for warmness-coldness** and **0.64 (CI 0.56–0.72) for wetness-dryness**[S2] — i.e., only moderate agreement even among experts.
- **Agreement study (Shiraz E-Medical Journal, ~350 healthcare workers; clinical assessment vs. two questionnaires, SMQ and MMQ):** highest agreement kappa **0.512** for hotness (clinical-vs-SMQ; 95% CI 0.422–0.602) and for coldness (clinical-vs-MMQ; 95% CI 0.368–0.656); **lowest kappa 0.081** for "balance in dryness/wetness" (95% CI −0.023 to 0.185); questionnaire-vs-questionnaire kappa 0.132–0.366. The authors concede "no independent external gold standard exists for Mizaj assessment."[S3]
- **Indian/Bangladeshi work (Mizaij-i-Insani questionnaire; J48 decision-tree studies on 68 subjects)** similarly report inter-rater judgment analyses and note "due to the lack of scientific validation, no standard method or tool is available to determine Mizaj."[S4]

**Interpretation:** By the standard Landis-Koch benchmarks, mizaj agreement is "slight" to "moderate" (kappa ~0.08–0.64), rarely reaching "substantial." Warm/cold is assessed more reliably than wet/dry. There is a clear scholarly admission that no gold standard exists. **I found no rigorous inter-rater reliability study of Unani *pulse* (nabz) diagnosis specifically** producing kappa values; the reliability literature clusters on mizaj questionnaires. (Searches performed: "Unani mizaj temperament inter-rater reliability kappa"; "pulse diagnosis reliability" within the mizaj/traditional-medicine literature. Pulse-diagnosis reliability data that do exist are largely from Traditional Chinese Medicine, not Unani.)

### 4. Colonialism, modernization, and "scientization"

Under the British Raj, Lord Macaulay's 1835 education policy privileged Western learning, and only Western medicine was recognized as legitimate; indigenous systems were "actively discouraged." Historians (Seema Alavi, Guy Attewell, Neshat Quaiser, Kavita Sivaramakrishnan) document how, paradoxically, colonial pressure and the arrival of print, vernacular (Urdu) publishing, and the public sphere (e.g., the *Oudh Akhbar* newspaper) transformed Unani from a family-/lineage-based elite practice into a public, professionalized, "modern" system.

Key actors and institutions:
- **Hakim Ajmal Khan (1868–1927):** the pivotal reformer-nationalist. He built the Ayurvedic and Unani Tibbia College in Karol Bagh, Delhi (foundation stone laid by Lord Hardinge 1916; inaugurated 1921 by Gandhi),[S5] the Hindustani Dawakhana, and the Central College. Crucially, he **proposed absorbing Western anatomy, surgery and biomedical concepts** into Unani — "diametrically opposite" to the purist Lucknow (Azizi family) school[S6] that wanted to preserve doctrinal purity. He was also President of the Indian National Congress (1921) and a leader of the Khilafat and Non-cooperation movements, tying Unani's survival to anti-colonial nationalism. He recognized the chemist Salimuzzaman Siddiqi, whose research on Unani medicinal plants gave the system a laboratory-science direction.
- **The Hamdard institutions:** Hakim Hafiz Abdul Majeed founded Hamdard as a small Unani clinic in Delhi in 1906 (Rooh Afza launched 1907). After Partition, Hakim Abdul Hameed (1908–1999) built Hamdard India (and Jamia Hamdard, a deemed university), while his brother Hakim Mohammed Said built Hamdard Pakistan (1948, Karachi). Both were constituted as *waqf* (endowments), industrializing and standardizing Unani pharmacy "along scientific lines."
- **Committees:** The Bhore Committee (Health Survey and Development Committee, reported 1946), dominated by the Indian Medical Service, dismissed indigenous medicine as "unscientific." The Chopra Committee (Committee on Indigenous Systems of Medicine, 1948) — chaired by R. N. Chopra and including hakims such as Nasiruddin Ahmed Khan and Hassan Qarshi — recommended integration/synthesis toward "one Indian system." Later committees (Mudaliar, Shrivastava, Udupa) oscillated between marginalization and integration.
- **Hyderabad:** The Nizams of Hyderabad were significant patrons of Unani, supporting institutions such as the Nizamia Tibbi College; Hyderabad remains a major Unani research center (home to the Central Research Institute of Unani Medicine, CRIUM). (This patronage detail rests on lighter sourcing — see Caveats.)

**What survivors changed to survive:** incorporation of anatomy, physiology, pathology and surgery into curricula; formal colleges with fixed syllabi and degrees; industrial pharmacy and standardized compound formulations; pharmacopoeial standardization; and a rhetorical "scientization" recasting Unani as rational and evidence-compatible. David Hardiman and others (following Charles Leslie and Romila Thapar's "syndication" concept) argue this amounted to *reinventing* Unani as a "cogent medical system with a well-defined structure."

### 5. Legal and professional standing today

**India (strongest institutionalization):**
- **National Commission for Indian System of Medicine (NCISM) Act, 2020**, which repealed the Indian Medicine Central Council Act, 1970[S7] and established the NCISM (operational June/July 2021), governing Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha and Sowa-Rigpa via a Board of Unani, Siddha and Sowa-Rigpa.[S8] It introduced common entrance (NEET) and exit (NExT) testing.
- **Ministry of AYUSH** (Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Homoeopathy) — the umbrella ministry.
- **CCRUM** (Central Council for Research in Unani Medicine, est. 1978)[S9] — the state research body; **National Institute of Unani Medicine, Bengaluru** — the apex teaching institute.
- **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 (Chapter IVA)** and Rules 1945 (Schedule T GMP; Rule 158B licensing) regulate Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani (ASU) drugs;[S10] the **Unani Pharmacopoeia of India** and **National Formulary of Unani Medicine** set standards.

**Pakistan:** The **Unani, Ayurvedic and Homoeopathic Practitioners Act, 1965** established the framework; the **National Council for Tibb (NCT)**[S11] registers hakims and accredits Bachelor of Eastern Medicine (BEMS) and diploma programs. Hamdard Foundation Pakistan (via WHO EMRO) states "there are ~60,000 registered Hakims in Pakistan holding either diploma in Tibb-e-Unani or a Bachelor degree in Eastern/Unani medicine"; a lower Ministry-of-Health-linked figure of 39,584 hakims (Saeed et al. 2011) also circulates. In September 2024 the government moved to merge the NCT and the National Council for Homeopathy into a National Council of Traditional, Alternative and Complementary Medicine.[S12]

**Bangladesh and Sri Lanka:** Unani is recognized and taught (e.g., Hamdard Laboratories (Waqf) Bangladesh, established in East Pakistan in 1953; government Unani boards). Sri Lanka regulates Ayurveda/Unani/Siddha under its indigenous-medicine framework.

**Iran ("Persian medicine" revival):** Formalized Traditional Persian Medicine (TPM) academic training was approved by Iran's Ministry of Health in 2007, creating a 4-year PhD program;[S13] the School of Persian Medicine at Tehran University of Medical Sciences is the oldest and largest;[S14] the Supreme Council of Cultural Revolution issued a national document supporting the revival;[S15] multiple TPM schools and dozens of TPM health centers/polyclinics (offering cupping, etc.) now operate, some services insurance-covered.

**South Africa:** Unani-Tibb was recognized in 2001 as the 11th modality under the **Allied Health Professions Council of South Africa (AHPCSA)** (Allied Health Professions Act 63 of 1982), with a practitioner register opened (2001, formally officiated September 2007) and training at the University of the Western Cape's School of Natural Medicine (curriculum developed with Hamdard Pakistan and CCRUM/CCIM support); SAHPRA recognizes Unani-Tibb as a complementary-medicine discipline for product regulation.[S16]

**Gulf/UK/US/EU:** Practice exists (often as cupping/herbal CAM) but generally without dedicated statutory recognition of "Unani" as a distinct licensed profession; products fall under general food-supplement/herbal or complementary-medicine regimes.

**WHO:** Published **WHO benchmarks for the training of Unani medicine** and **WHO benchmarks for the practice of Unani medicine** (both 11 February 2022, updating 2010 editions), plus standardized Unani terminology — normative reference documents for member states.

### 6. Clinical research: who produces it and what rigorous reviews conclude

The research output is dominated by CCRUM and Indian Unani colleges, Hamdard, and Iranian TPM universities. Volume is substantial but methodological quality is consistently poor:
- **Trial designs** are frequently single-arm, open-label, or small non-inferiority RCTs (e.g., a non-inferiority RCT of a Unani formulation vs. PUVA for chronic plaque psoriasis, IJMR 2018; a 33-patient single-arm psoriasis trial at CRIUM Hyderabad 2018–19;[S17] a rheumatoid-arthritis non-inferiority RCT vs. celecoxib).
- **Systematic reviews** of Unani (e.g., post-COVID-19 symptom management) repeatedly report that only a handful of studies meet inclusion criteria, with small samples (n=60–100), observational designs, and "high risk in allocation" bias on the Cochrane tool,[S18] concluding evidence is "promising" but insufficient.
- **Cupping/hijama** has the largest procedural evidence base. Systematic reviews (AlBedah 2011; Kim et al.; Cha et al. evidence map 2021) find *some* low-to-moderate-quality positive signals for pain (low back pain, neck pain, knee osteoarthritis)[S19]. One 2024 meta-analysis (Zhang, Pasapula, Wang, Edwards & Norrish, *Complementary Therapies in Medicine* 2024;80:103013; 11 trials, 921 participants) reported that "high-quality evidence demonstrated cupping significantly improves pain at 2–8 weeks endpoint intervention (d=1.09, 95% CI: 0.35–1.83, p=0.004)" — but found no significant improvement at 1 month (d=0.11) or 3–6 months (d=0.39), and reviewers uniformly note small trials, weak blinding (sham-cupping is hard), and heterogeneity. Wet-cupping-for-mental-health reviews found most trials "of poor quality."[S20]
- **Publication pattern:** strong positive-result bias, much output in low-impact or in-house journals, geographic clustering (India, Iran, China for cupping), and weak independent replication.

**Bottom line:** No Unani intervention currently rests on high-certainty, independently replicated evidence of clinically meaningful efficacy. Cupping for musculoskeletal pain is the least-weak case, and even there the benefit appears short-lived; most herbal formulations remain at the preliminary/hypothesis-generating stage.

### 7. Documented harms and regulatory response

- **Heavy metals in kushta (metallic calx) and surma (kohl) preparations:** These are the best-documented Unani-specific harms. Elemental analysis of the Unani formulation **Kushtae Sadaf** (National Institute of Unani Medicine, Bengaluru) found lead at **28.7 ppm in one marketed sample — above the WHO/India 10 ppm limit**[S21] — while another sample was within limits, showing batch/manufacturer variability. **Surma/kohl (ithmid/galena)** preparations have contained lead "as high as 86%" (Aslam M, Davis SS, Healy MA, *Public Health* 1979;93(5):274–284: "Some samples of Surma were found to have a lead content as high as 86%"), and a Rawalpindi/Islamabad JPMA study (Haq & Khan, *JPMA* 1982;32(1), 40 samples) found lead "from 0.03% to 81.37%" — enough to cause chronic lead poisoning with regular use; South Asian immigrant lead-poisoning clusters in the UK were traced to surma.[S22] A CCRUM/RRIUM review (Mustehasan & Azhar, *Asian J Res Chem* 2022;15(5):358–366) confirms that multiple lead compounds (Usrub, Isfidaj, Suranj, Murdar sang, Ithmid) and arsenic trioxide (Kushta Sammulfar) are **formularized Unani ingredients**, given orally as kushtas — with the authors themselves acknowledging toxicity (e.g., Ithmid "not recommended for oral use due to toxic effects"; Murdar sang oral intake "is harmful and can cause dysuria").
- **Undeclared allopathic drugs (steroids):** A JPMA study (1981) found **80% of 29 tested Unani and homeopathic samples contained corticosteroids** (cortisone, hydrocortisone, dexamethasone, prednisolone),[S23] explaining dramatic "cures" in arthritis/asthma. A 2026 analytical study of the classical Unani formulation **Jawarish Shahi** (*Discover Chemistry*) detected synthetic contaminants including an antibiotic prodrug (single-batch preliminary finding).
- **Hijama (wet cupping) infections/adverse events:** Documented case reports include disseminated *Staphylococcus aureus* with multi-organ injury after scarification cupping (PMC10061885), sacroiliac abscess/osteomyelitis,[S24] necrotizing fasciitis (PMC8059418), and a reported death after consecutive-day hijama by an unlicensed practitioner (*J Forensic Leg Med* 2025). Reviews attribute these to non-sterile technique and untrained practitioners.
- **Hepatotoxicity:** Unani-*specific*, product-named liver-injury case reports are scarce; most traditional-medicine DILI reports name Ayurvedic agents. Unani appears in mixed-system DILI surveys as a regulated-but-under-monitored category, and its metallic preparations carry inherent hepato/nephro/neurotoxic potential (the CCRUM Usrub review itself notes lead distributes to liver, kidney and brain, causing encephalopathy, neuropathy and nephropathy).
- **Delayed biomedical care** is a recognized systemic risk of reliance on traditional medicine, though hard Unani-specific outcome data are limited.

**Regulatory handling:** India's Department of AYUSH order (F. No. K-11020/5/97-DCC, 14 October 2005) mandated heavy-metal testing (As, Pb, Hg, Cd) of ASU drugs for export from 1 January 2006, with "Heavy Metals within permissible limits" labelling;[S25] permissible limits (Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India Part-I Vol-VII, 2008) are **lead 10 ppm, arsenic 3 ppm, mercury 1 ppm, cadmium 0.3 ppm** for herbal products.[S26] **A critical loophole:** intentionally metallic preparations (rasa-shastra bhasmas and, by analogy, Unani kushtas) are assessed under separate criteria rather than simply failed against the 10 ppm herbal ceiling.[S26] A National Pharmacovigilance Programme for ASU drugs was launched (2008), lapsed for lack of support, and was later reconstituted.[S27] WHO guidance and the FAO/WHO limits provide the international backdrop; a 2023 nine-pharmacopoeia comparison found ASU pharmacopoeias sit outside mainstream harmonization.[S28]

### 8. "Invented tradition" vs. continuity

This is the liveliest historiographic debate, and the weight of specialist scholarship favors a **"reconstruction on a real foundation"** position:
- **Reconstruction/"invented tradition" side:** **Guy Attewell** (*Refiguring Unani Tibb: Plural Healing in Late Colonial India*, 2007) shows Unani's identity was "reconstituted" around 1900 through biomedical concepts, nationalist/communitarian politics, and colonial models of legitimacy, and that it was heterogeneous rather than a single "traditional Muslim system."[S29] **Seema Alavi** (*Islam and Healing: Loss and Recovery of an Indo-Muslim Medical Tradition, 1600–1900*, 2007; and "Unani Medicine in the Nineteenth-Century Public Sphere," IESHR 2005) traces how a "new Unani and a new hakim" emerged in the 19th-century Urdu public sphere, with emphasis shifting from theory-mastery to practice.[S30] **Claudia Liebeskind** ("Arguing Science: Unani Tibb, Hakims and Biomedicine in India, 1900–50," in Ernst ed., 2002) documents the biomedicine-vs-hakim contest. **David Hardiman** explicitly frames both Unani and Ayurveda as reinvented/syndicated in colonial India. **Kavita Sivaramakrishnan** (*Old Potions, New Bottles: Recasting Indigenous Medicine in Colonial Punjab*, 2006) shows analogous recasting. For Iran, critics argue the "Persian medicine" (TPM) revival since 2007 is a substantially new, nationally branded construction layered onto medieval texts. Kira Schmidt Stiedenroth (*Unani Medicine in the Making*, 2020) extends this to 21st-century India.
- **Continuity side:** Defenders (many within CCRUM, Hamdard, TPM faculties, and bio-bibliographers such as the late Hakim Syed Zillur Rahman) rest on the genuinely unbroken textual lineage (Galen→Qanun→Indian commentaries), continuous family/lineage practice (e.g., the Azizi family of Lucknow), and manuscript evidence.
- **What each rests on:** the reconstruction side rests on 19th–20th-c. print, institutional, legal and political archives; the continuity side rests on the textual/manuscript tradition and lineage practice. **Speziale's** work (below) complicates both by showing pre-colonial dynamism (Persian-Ayurveda synthesis) — undercutting the idea of a static "pure" tradition that was either simply preserved or simply invented.

### 9. Shared features with neighbouring traditions — borrowing vs. convergence

- **Greek/Galenic European medicine:** *demonstrated common descent* (same Hippocratic-Galenic humoral source; the Qanun was itself a Latin-European textbook until the 17th century) — this is shared ancestry, not borrowing.
- **Ayurveda (and Siddha):** *demonstrated borrowing, textually attested.* Indian physicians (e.g., Mankah al-Hindi, 8th c.) worked at the Abbasid court, translating Sanskrit medical texts; Indian drugs entered Arabic/Persian pharmacopoeias. **Fabrizio Speziale** (EHESS; *Culture persane et médecine ayurvédique en Asie du Sud*, Brill 2018; the Perso-Indica project) documents a large movement, from the 14th century onward, translating Ayurvedic (Sanskrit) sources into Persian[S31] and "Persianising" Ayurvedic therapeutics — and a symmetric Persianization of Hindu physicians who learned Persian. Pharmacology was "the field in which the contacts with the local tradition determined the most sensible renewal," as Muslim physicians incorporated Indian materia medica to adapt practice to local conditions. This is documented incorporation, not mere parallel evolution.
- **Prophetic medicine (Tibb al-Nabawi):** partly *incorporated* in colonial India (Attewell/Alavi note Sufi and Prophetic healing practices were folded into vernacular Unani), though scholars treat Tibb al-Nabawi and academic Unani as historically distinct strands.
- **Chinese and Tibetan medicine:** *convergence* on shared concepts (cupping; qualitative/energetic frameworks; pulse examination) with limited direct textual borrowing into Unani; Sowa-Rigpa (Tibetan) is now administratively grouped with Unani under India's NCISM but is a separate tradition. Cupping's transmission is documented from Greco-Roman/Byzantine practice into the Arab-Muslim world (so hijama's lineage is Greco-Arabic, and its resemblance to Chinese cupping is largely convergent/parallel).

### 10. What specialists are currently fighting about

- **Evidence standards and integration:** whether Unani should be validated by RCT/biomedical standards at all, or whether its holistic/individualized logic requires different methods — an unresolved methodological fault line.
- **AYUSH "mixopathy"/"crosspathy":** the most heated Indian policy fight. The Indian Medical Association has repeatedly protested NCISM/IMCC moves allowing AYUSH (including Unani) graduates to perform surgery, prescribe modern drugs, or train in integrated MBBS-BAMS-type courses, calling it unscientific "mixopathy" that endangers patients; state governments (Maharashtra's CCMP scheme; Andhra Pradesh's December 2025 approval of 58 surgical procedures for PG Ayurvedic doctors) have pushed integration, with litigation pending before the Bombay High Court and Supreme Court. A parallel dispute concerns whether AYUSH practitioners may use the "Dr" prefix.
- **Persian medicine revival (Iran):** debate over whether the state-backed TPM program is legitimate scientific revival or nationalist "invented tradition," and over integrating it into insurance and the health system.
- **COVID-19 claims:** during the pandemic, AYUSH/Unani immunity and treatment claims drew criticism for outpacing evidence; post-COVID Unani reviews acknowledge weak evidence and small, biased studies.
- **Standardization, nomenclature, and safety:** disputes over drug standardization, heavy-metal limits and the kushta/bhasma carve-out, WHO terminology, and quality control.

## Recommendations

**For a researcher/clinician evaluating Unani claims (staged):**
1. **Treat the history as solid but distinguish "the texts" from "the modern system."** Cite Attewell (2007), Alavi (2007), Liebeskind (2002), Speziale (2018) for the reconstruction thesis; cite Hunayn's Risala (Lamoreaux 2016 edition) and the Qanun manuscript scholarship (Historia Medica; Janssens) for the textual lineage. Do not accept "1,000 years of unbroken practice exactly as today" claims uncritically.
2. **When assessing diagnostic reliability, cite the actual kappa/ICC values** (mizaj ICC ~0.62–0.64; kappa ranges 0.08–0.82) and state plainly that agreement is slight-to-moderate and that no gold standard exists. Flag the absence of Unani pulse-diagnosis reliability data.
3. **For efficacy, default to systematic reviews, not individual CCRUM/college trials.** Rate cupping-for-musculoskeletal-pain as the least-weak case (short-term only); treat herbal formulations as preliminary. Demand independent replication, adequate blinding, and pre-registration before upgrading any recommendation.
4. **On safety, insist on heavy-metal certificates for any kushta/mineral/surma preparation** and screen for undeclared steroids in "fast-acting" anti-inflammatory Unani products. Counsel patients about hijama infection risk and about delayed care.

**Thresholds that would change these positions:** a complete critical edition of the Qanun (would sharpen textual debates); a large multi-center inter-rater study showing mizaj kappa >0.6 consistently (would strengthen diagnostic credibility); adequately powered, pre-registered, independently replicated double-blind RCTs with low risk of bias showing clinically meaningful benefit (would justify specific clinical recommendations); and enforced, loophole-free heavy-metal limits with post-market surveillance data (would materially reduce the documented harm profile).

## Caveats

- **Source asymmetry:** Much clinical and pharmacological literature is produced by institutions with an institutional stake in Unani (CCRUM, Hamdard, TPM faculties), and promotional/marketing sources (school and clinic websites) overstate historical continuity and efficacy. I have weighted peer-reviewed history, systematic reviews, primary legal texts, and regulatory documents accordingly.
- **"Absence of evidence" items explicitly searched:** (a) I found **no rigorous kappa-based inter-rater reliability study of Unani pulse (nabz) diagnosis** — the reliability literature is on mizaj questionnaires. (b) Unani-*specific*, product-named hepatotoxicity case reports with RUCAM scoring appear **absent** in the indexed literature (the DILI literature names Ayurvedic agents; Unani appears only in mixed-system surveys). (c) Clinical case reports attributing an individual's lead/arsenic poisoning to a specific *branded* Unani kushta (with attributed blood levels) are **sparse** compared with Ayurveda; the strongest Unani harm evidence is product-analysis (over-limit metals) plus surma-linked poisoning clusters.
- **Terminology overlap:** Toxicology and pharmacopoeial sources frequently aggregate "Ayurveda, Siddha and Unani (ASU)," so some cited limits/surveys are ASU-wide rather than Unani-only; I have flagged this where relevant.
- **Lighter sourcing:** Detail on Nizam-of-Hyderabad patronage and on COVID-era Unani specifics rests on thinner sourcing (searches on these were cut off by tool limits) and should be verified against primary sources before heavy reliance; the harms question was filled by a dedicated sub-investigation and is well-sourced.
- **Fast-moving policy:** The Indian "mixopathy" litigation, the Pakistani council merger (2024), and Iran's TPM expansion are ongoing; legal specifics may have changed after the sources' dates.
