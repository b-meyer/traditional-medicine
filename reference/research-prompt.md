Research **[SYSTEM]** as a medical tradition and answer the questions below as fully as the evidence allows. Where the evidence does not exist, say that clearly and say what you looked for — that answer is as valuable to me as a positive one.

1. Where does this tradition come from, what are its foundational texts, and how much do specialists actually agree about their dating, authorship, and transmission?
2. How does it explain health and illness on its own terms, and where does that framework map onto biomedical categories and where does it refuse to?
3. How does a practitioner arrive at a diagnosis — and if two practitioners examine the same patient, how often do they reach the same conclusion?
4. What happened to this tradition under colonialism and modernization, who fought to preserve or abolish it, and what did the survivors change in order to survive?
5. What is its legal and professional standing today, in which countries, and under what specific laws?
6. How much clinical research exists, who produced it, and what do the most rigorous reviews conclude about which of its interventions?
7. What harm has it been documented to cause, and how has that been handled?
8. Who argues that the modern form of this tradition is substantially a recent invention, who argues for continuity, and what does each side rest on?
9. What does it share with neighbouring traditions, and which similarities are demonstrated borrowing rather than convergence?
10. What are specialists currently fighting about?

Answer in this order, in prose.

Rules, applying throughout:

- Any quantitative claim carries its study, sample size, and effect or agreement figure. No bare assertions of efficacy or scale.
- Any regulatory or legal claim names the instrument, the jurisdiction, and the year. "Officially recognized" is not a claim.
- Anything you describe as contested names specialists on both sides and states their positions.
- Keep three registers visibly separate and never let one vouch for another: the tradition's account of itself, historical scholarship about it, and biomedical evidence about its interventions.
- How much research exists and what that research concludes are separate questions. Answer them separately. Never let "little studied" read as "found ineffective."
- Use your sources' own hedging. Do not sharpen a low-certainty conclusion into a confident one, and report GRADE or equivalent certainty ratings where reviews give them.
- Prefer, in order: systematic reviews and meta-analyses; scholarly reference works and monographs from academic presses; institutional documents from regulators and the WHO; peer-reviewed primary studies. Name the source type for significant claims.
- Flag any significant claim resting on a single source, any place where sources conflict, and any area where the scholarship comes mainly from institutions invested in this tradition's legitimacy.

Close with an account of what you searched for and where you came up empty.
